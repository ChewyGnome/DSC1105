Dataset

The dataset that will be used in this report is the 2023 Yellow Taxi Trip Records provided by the NYC Taxi & Limousine Commission (TLC). In this dataset, we have the data regarding the millions of yellow taxi trips throughout the year of 2023. Containing specific details such as the drop off times, trip distance, total fare amount, and etc. The objective of this study is to provide a model function that will calculate the accurate conversion for fare fee to the amount of distance travelled; preferably also taking into account the seasonal differences across fares.

Datasource: https://www.nyc.gov/site/tlc/about/tlc-trip-record-data.page

Part 2: Classical Linear Regression

Model Fit

lm_model <- lm(total_amount ~ trip_distance, data = taxi_sample)

tidy(lm_model) %>% knitr::kable(digits = 3)

  knitr::kable(digits = 4)
r.squared
0.0582

Interpretation:

The fitted model is: \[\text{total\_amount} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \times \text{trip\_distance} + \varepsilon\]

Slope (\(\beta_1\)):

For each mile travelled, the fare cost also increases in proportion to the distance increase

Intercept (\(\beta_0\)):

Represents the base fare when distance is 0. While R² indicates that trip distance shows a substantial portion of variability in the total fare.

R²:

The R² value suggest that there is a proportional increase between total amount and trip distance. With having higher yields of R² value show a stronger explanatory power of the linear model.

Residuals vs Fitted Plot:

plot(lm_model$fitted.values,
     lm_model$residuals,
     xlab = "Fitted Values",
     ylab = "Residuals",
     main = "Residuals vs Fitted")
abline(h = 0, col = "red")

Diagnostics Comments:

Linearity:

If the residuals are randomly scattered around zero without a clear pattern, the linearity assumption is reasonably satisfied. In the case that curvature appears, this would suggest a nonlinear relationship between trip_distance and total_amount.

Heteroskedasticity:

If the spread of residuals increases as fitted values increase, this indicates heteroskedasticity (non-constant variance). This is more common in datas where longer trips show greater variability in total charges.

Part 6: Reflection

  1. When is linear modeling inadequate?

  2. Why use flexible regression?

  3. How do outliers affect interpretation in large datasets?

Linear modeling proves to be an inadequate analytical tool in circumstances and examples that don’t have a consistent trend of increase/decrease. It falls short on situations where one would need to make baseless assumptions that may lead to disagreements between different parties.

By enabling the model to adjust to intricate, non-linear patterns in the data, flexible regression techniques such as polynomial terms, splines, generalized additive models (GAMs), or fully nonlinear approaches overcome these drawbacks. Better approximations of real-world relationships that linear assumptions oversimplify are made possible by this increased flexibility, which frequently results in lower bias, better predictive accuracy, and more explanatory power.

In large datasets, outliers—extreme values far from the main cluster—still influence linear regression, though their relative impact diminishes as sample size grows. In small samples, a single outlier can dramatically skew the regression line, inflating slope estimates, increasing standard errors, and distorting coefficients.
